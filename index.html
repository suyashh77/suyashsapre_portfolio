E1

Lowe’s — Electrical & Lighting services RFP

Ran a ~$10–16M/yr services RFP end to end, and built the spend model and scenario tool that turned a two-week award debate into one meeting.

Scope

Owned the Electrical & Lighting services RFP for Lowe’s Retail Facilities: every store and owned supply-chain site, about $10–16M a year, three-year fixed pricing for 2026–2029. I sourced new suppliers, ran the event end to end in Coupa, led the project meetings with the business unit, and built the models behind the award recommendation.

What I built

  • Projected-spend model. Forecast work orders from a weighted three-year history (0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2) instead of reusing last year’s count, then priced labor, lifts, material and trip fees per supplier and state. The business unit approved it as the basis for the award.
  • Scenario tool. Replaced four linked spreadsheets — where one scenario took 10–15 minutes and one broken formula broke the rest — with a single Python model and map dashboard. Scenario comparisons ran in under a minute, and award discussions expected to take about two weeks closed in one meeting. It was used live when the business unit pushed back on North Carolina coverage.
  • Supplier performance. Indexed six service KPIs per supplier (category average = 100). The standout was a small, self-performing supplier: first-time fix 114, cost per work order 78. Parts-on-order tracked closely with work-order age, which pointed to van-stocking discipline as the driver.

The decision

I framed four award scenarios indexed to plan = 100. I rejected the cheapest (88) because it handed an unproven supplier a third of the country, and recommended 96. The business unit chose 100, which favoured a supplier with a proven storm-response record — reliability my cost index didn’t capture.

Results

Award landed on plan, contracts signed by mid-August. Supplier base went from 6 to 9, cutting concentration risk. The worst performer’s store coverage fell 45% and the best performer’s grew 50%.

What didn’t work

I built, then shut down, an LLM-assisted should-cost model: with no validated parts lists for repairs, it produced precise-looking numbers on unreliable inputs. And my van-stocking requirement didn’t make the contract — I raised it late and sent no example parts list, so suppliers answered generically. Next time: put supplier-facing requirements in front of suppliers, with a template, before the bid goes live.

UPL — Warehouse network & SKU placement

A Python network tool over 63,512 orders that tested a 30 → 9 warehouse consolidation: 93.3% of orders still within 600 miles.

Scope

Three co-op rotations in supply chain and manufacturing operations, during a plan to cut active finished-goods warehouses from 30 to 9. The question: can nine warehouses still put the right product near the customer?

What I built

  • Network analysis tool. A refreshable Python program over 63,512 orders. For each order it finds the SKU’s planned warehouses and ships from the nearest eligible one, then checks reach, compares SAP inventory against the plan, and recommends import locations. Refresh three input files and rerun — so it works on forecasts as well as history.
  • SKU segmentation. Split 513 SKUs by how many states buy them. 379 regional SKUs are about 17% of volume but 30% of revenue; 26 broad SKUs are 55% of volume but 24% of revenue. Premium regional products and mass-market products need different placement rules.
  • Master data. Corrected BOMs for 892 SKUs with standard component-loss rates, created or updated 246 finishing specs, and standardized brand naming across 15,215 SKU-plant records in SAP.

Results

With real SKU-to-warehouse constraints, the nine-warehouse network reached 93.3% of orders and about 98% of revenue within 600 miles. The long-distance remainder traced to specific causes — Hawaii, specially stored products, rare one-off sales — not to a missing warehouse.

E3

Lenovo — SmartPack fulfillment & the SmartPull prototype

Timed 94 rack stops with an app I built, traced the delays to stale information, and prototyped SmartPull — a live task queue for pullers.

Why

Lenovo’s Whitsett, NC distribution center ships same-day orders on one 7:30 PM truck. Our team of five was asked to cut order-to-shipment time in SmartPack — the area that pulls, kits and packs those orders — by at least 10%, with no added headcount. I was the team’s data analyst: I owned pulling, built our timing app and the prototypes, and led most of the meetings with Lenovo.

How we measured

Eight site visits and about 20 hours on the floor. Stopwatches and four people’s notes don’t reconcile, so I built a tablet timer app: every observer taps the same standard elements, with automatic timestamps and one-tap exception codes. With it we timed 94 rack stops across six operators, 25 kitted pallets (393 boxes), 52 packing observations and 15 kickouts, and tested differences between equipment and drivers statistically rather than by eye.

What we found

The physical work was good. The information it ran on wasn’t. Fishbone, 5 Whys and FMEA came down to four root causes:

  • Old. The pull sheet is a printed snapshot of SAP, up to two hours stale by the time it’s worked.
  • Private. Routes and bin corrections live in people’s heads or on one paper sheet.
  • Not shared. Extra stock from earlier picks has no list and no owner, and nobody sees an order’s progress against the truck until packing.
  • Not built in. Pulling methods depend on habit, and drivers differed significantly from each other.

The worst pulling delays came from wrong and empty locations, not from walking speed.

What we recommended

21 candidate fixes across pulling, kitting and packing, scored in a weighted decision matrix (cycle time, feasibility, investment, operating cost, labor, data readiness). The best pulling fixes are cheap: a 5S’d overflow area with an owner, one pulling standard, a pre-trip bin check against SAP, and a shared discrepancy log. They’re sequenced as a phased roadmap — Standardize → Digitize → Execute → Extend — with a pilot gate between phases, so Lenovo funds the next step only after the last one is measured. Every design was checked against OSHA 1910.178 and 1910.176, the NIOSH lifting equation, GS1 barcode standards and ISO 9001. Payback is left as a formula for Lenovo to fill with its own labor rate; we make no savings claims.

SmartPull — the prototype I built

The Execute phase replaces the paper sheet with a live task queue. Each puller gets one task at a time: scan the bin, scan the part, next task. Stock is reserved when a task is issued, a wrong or empty bin is one tap that sends a count task and re-routes, and each load is sequenced by an exact route solver. A Control Tower shows every order’s ETA against the 7:30 PM truck, so a lead sees a late order mid-afternoon instead of at 7:31.

Try the SmartPull demo →

For kitting, a two-button scan check: scan the pick sheet, scan the part, green means go. It catches what eyes miss on small labels — swapped digits, a letter O for a zero.

Status

Measure and Analyze are complete; the course ends in December with the recommendations and a pilot plan. Nothing has been implemented, so no results are claimed. Lenovo’s performance data, volumes and cycle times stay with Lenovo under NDA, and the demo runs on example data.

R1

Statewide food-access dataset — 1.9M grocery reviews

1.94M Google Maps reviews from 4,442 stores in all 100 NC counties — after catching a bias that would have invented rural food deserts.

Why

Most food-access research measures distance to a store. Teresa’s Raleigh pilot measured access through what people write in Google Maps reviews, across Penchansky & Thomas’s five dimensions — availability, accessibility, accommodation, affordability, acceptability — for 49 stores. My job was to scale it to the whole state. I own collection, store identity and quality control.

What went wrong first

The first collection built its store list from a single map database. My audit found it kept only 14–38% of candidate stores depending on the region, lowest in rural eastern NC — a list that would make food deserts appear where the gap was in the data, not on the ground. It also had wrong-location scrapes: one Hendersonville Sam’s Club held 1,062 reviews belonging to a Raleigh store. We threw that collection out and rebuilt.

How it works now

  • A store frame from four independent sources — USDA SNAP, Overture Maps, OpenStreetMap and project records — matched with a tiered rule and with every scope ruling logged with a reason.
  • Five validation gates before freezing. Capture–recapture puts frame coverage of core grocery stores at no more than 98.5%, and every one of 8,883 SNAP retailers links to a frame store.
  • A scraper that can’t silently be wrong: it opens each store by its Google place ID and rejects any page that doesn’t match, checks that every scroll loaded new reviews, records provenance on each review, and resumes after crashes. 25 student volunteers ran an early version; their complaints became version 2.

What it produced

99.1% of stores are complete under the completion rule, with 91× the stores and 39× the text reviews of the pilot. Reviews attributed to the wrong store were quarantined with a reason rather than deleted, and the database is frozen with a checksum so every count above can be recomputed.

Limits: half the reviews have no text, and 89% carry only a year-level date. Stores with more than 1,500 reviews hold their newest reviews, not a random sample. This phase builds the data; scoring the five dimensions is the next one.

Now — Phase 2

An embedding pipeline that takes the pilot’s review-text scoring statewide, measuring the five access dimensions for every store from what people wrote. Labels are provisional until a human topic check is in, so no results yet.

R2

GeoMatch — open-source entity resolution

Offline, explainable store matching: 0.94 recall at 95% precision, nearly double standard fuzzy matching.

Why

Building that dataset, I kept hitting the same problem: a list of stores from one source and a list from another, and no way to tell which rows were the same real place. 

"Food Lion #1542, 2410 Wade Ave Ste 100"   same store as the next one
"FOOD LION 2410 WADE AVE RALEIGH NC 27607"
"Food Lion Pharmacy, 2410 Wade Avenue"     a department of it, not the same place
"Dollar Tree, 2410 Wade Ave"               a different business at the same address

The existing options either wanted an API key — so the addresses leave your machine, which you can’t do with someone else’s research data — or depended on a C library that’s painful to install. So I wrote one that runs offline, with no LLM, and installs anywhere.

How it works

Layers that fail in different ways, so that no single signal can force a decision.

  • Take the address apart. House number, street, suffix, unit, city, ZIP — following the post office’s own rules, so Ave and Avenue, or NC-54 and NC Hwy 54, become the same thing.
  • Understand the name. Resolve the brand, read store numbers, and recognise departments (pharmacy, fuel center) so a store’s pharmacy isn’t matched as the store.
  • Find candidates several ways at once — nearby, same house number, same brand, shared rare word — so two records whose ZIPs disagree still get compared.
  • Score the evidence. Each family (address, locality, name, distance) gets a weight learned from labeled NC pairs; missing data counts as neutral, not as a mismatch.
  • Say why. Every pair comes back as match / uncertain / non-match, a relation (same, part of, different) and a plain-English reason built from its biggest weights.

What it found

On a held-out set of 495 labeled NC store pairs, GeoMatch reached 0.94 recall at precision ≥ 0.95 (precision 0.96, recall 0.91 at the default threshold), against 0.50 for standard fuzzy token-set matching and 0.47 for TF-IDF n-grams. On different businesses sharing one address — the case that motivated version 2 — precision was 1.00. On a public New York restaurant benchmark it was never tuned on: precision 1.00, recall 0.94.

Version 1 had hard reject rules — different ZIP or house number, rejected. When I measured them on real labels, they were throwing away about 4% of true matches, so version 2 removed them. I also tested a neural model; plain fuzzy features beat it (F1 0.85 vs 0.78), so it isn’t in the core.

EvidenceMeasured — 495 held-out NC pairs (labels model-annotated) plus public benchmarks

01

Facility location

Same 8 candidate fire stations, different answers per objective — cost, coverage and worst-case models side by side.

Why

At UPL I worked on a warehouse network redesign. In school, our team advised the City of Goldsboro on fire station siting: 8 candidate sites, 2,724 demand points, real drive-time matrices and a hard 4-minute first-truck standard. The lesson from both: “optimal” depends on which objective you picked. Minimise total distance, minimise the worst response, or maximise people covered inside 4 minutes — same 8 candidates, different stations open. That choice belongs to the decision-maker, so the job is to show them the trade-off.

How it works

Four families of model over the same candidate set, so the answers sit side by side: cost (UFLP, capacitated CFLP, p-median), coverage (set covering, maximum coverage, p-center), greedy heuristics benchmarked against the exact integer program, and a multi-objective sweep that traces the trade-off frontier and flags the weights where the open-station set flips. The capacitated model surfaces the capacity premium — the extra distance overflow demand travels once a station’s cap binds.

02

Freight consolidation & truck loading

Waiting for full trucks halves cost per cwt ($3.83 vs $7.44) — but one shipment waited 30.8 days.

Why

A consolidation centre makes two decisions that interact. When do you release a truck? Holding freight fills trailers and cuts cost, but every shipment on the dock is a shipment not at the DC. What goes on the truck? A trailer has a weight ceiling and a cube ceiling, and freight that fills one leaves the other slack. I simulated both on the same freight to see where consolidation pays and where it stops paying.

How it works

Freight is priced off a realistic LTL rate structure, because that makes or breaks everything else: rates drop at weight breaks, and a shipment can be billed at a heavier break when that’s cheaper — a 4,800 lb shipment moves as 5,000 lb and costs 23% less. Model freight as linear in weight and every conclusion about consolidation is worthless.

  • Dispatch: a fixed schedule, a fill threshold, or whichever comes first.
  • Load build: four packing heuristics, including residual_fit, which picks the shipment whose weight-and-cube profile best matches the room the trailer has left — the formal version of mixing dense and light freight on one load.
  • Break-even: shipping every supplier direct against pooling through a centre, swept across supplier count, shipment size and geography — with choices deliberately made against consolidation, so it can’t win on a penalty I invented.

What it found

Waiting is worth a lot, and the price is a long tail. Shipping to a fill threshold instead of a clock costs $3.83/cwt against $7.44/cwt (95% CI for the difference [−3.74, −3.48], cheaper on all 30 paired freight streams) for 1.68 extra days of mean dwell. But the worst shipment waits 30.8 days while p95 is 6.0 — a pure fill rule has no upper bound on how long an awkward shipment can sit. That’s the argument for a hybrid rule, and it shows up in no average.

Packer Trailers / 1,000 shipments Weight util Cube util
residual_fit — adaptive 22.73 0.901 0.856
ffd_scalarized(0.5) — fixed 50/50 blend 22.99 0.891 0.846

The adaptive packer uses 13.5% fewer trailers than packing heaviest-first and hits the theoretical lower bound on every instance. But my own claim that no fixed rule could match it didn’t hold: a flat 50/50 blend of weight and cube captures 93% of the gain.

Which ceiling binds flips with the freight mix. Trailers pin against weight or cube, almost never both. The flip happens at a mean freight density of 11.9 lb/ft³ — against the trailer’s own break-even density of 45,000 lb / 3,800 ft³ = 11.84. That isn’t a fitted number; it’s the trailer’s geometry showing up in the data.

More findings — weight breaks, fragmentation, break-even

Weight breaks don’t drive the release decision, which I didn’t expect. Outbound trailers are almost always cheaper as full truckloads, so cost per cwt tracks 1/fill almost exactly (correlation 0.9993 across 510 runs). The rate staircase binds on the inbound leg instead, where billing at a heavier break applies to 32.7% of tenders and takes 4.6% off the freight bill.

Fragmentation lands entirely on the inbound leg. Moving from a few dominant suppliers to many equal ones at the same total volume raises inbound cost by +$4.94/cwt (+19.3%); outbound fill doesn’t move.

The cost breakdown changed how I think about this: the centre’s own fixed cost ($54.67/cwt) was the largest line, larger than all the freight it handles, because at 7,400 lb/day it’s badly under-used. A siting decision should start with throughput, not with rates.

The rate table’s structure is representative of published LTL tariffs, but no figure in it comes from a real carrier, and its linear mileage scaling makes truckload too cheap at low weights — a documented simplification carried into the findings. Routing — TSP & VRP, exact vs heuristic

Exact solving proves a 150-stop TSP in 21 s but stalls on 15-customer VRPs, and the better starting route gives the worse final route.

Why

The truck is loaded. What order does it visit the stops in, and how many trucks do you need? I wanted to find out for myself where exact solving stops being worth the wait.

How it works

Three layers on the same 150 instances (10 to 100 stops, 15 seeds, uniform and clustered layouts): construction heuristics (nearest neighbour, nearest insertion, Clarke-Wright, sweep), local search on top (2-opt, Or-opt, moves between routes), and exact integer programs that add violated constraints as cuts until the answer is a valid set of routes.

What it found

The same cutting-plane idea behaves completely differently on the two problems: it proves a 150-stop TSP optimal in about 21 seconds, while the vehicle-routing model fails to prove optimality on 43% of 15-customer instances inside 45 seconds. Drop the subtour constraints from a TSP and what’s left is still tour-shaped; drop the capacity constraints from a VRP and what’s left barely resembles routing. So: solve a TSP exactly. Past about fifteen customers, use the exact VRP model to calibrate the heuristics on small cases, then trust them on large ones.

The result I didn’t expect: the better starting route produces the worse final route. Nearest insertion beats nearest neighbour on its own (19.9% vs 24.2% above optimal at 100 stops), but after identical local search it reverses, 4.47% vs 3.22%. Nearest neighbour’s flaw is a few long crossing edges — exactly what 2-opt removes. Judge a heuristic by what it leaves for the search.

04

Return routing

+$5.20 per return by deciding where it goes before it ships — and 90% of that is policy, not the machine learning.

Why

I returned an unworn jacket in the wrong size, and it still got shipped across the country so someone could open the box and decide it was resellable. Everything needed to make that call was knowable before the box moved. Most retailers route every return to a central warehouse and decide afterwards, losing shipping cost and value on the way. So: decide where a return goes before it ships.

How it works

  • L1 — predicts the item’s likely condition from what’s known when the return label is printed. I chose a calibrated logistic model over a slightly more accurate but badly calibrated random forest, because the next layer needs honest probabilities.
  • L2 — prices every possible destination in expected dollars: EV(j) = Σₖ P(k) × [R(j,k) − T(j) − P(j,k)]
  • L3 — an integer program assigns the day’s returns across a 58-location network under each site’s capacity.

What it found

+$5.20 recovered per return over the nearest-DC baseline (paired 95% CI [+$4.92, +$5.48]), about $520,000 a year at 100,000 returns, capturing 83% of what a perfect-information router would.

Then I switched the machine learning off. Free in-store returns plus capacity-aware routing account for 90% of the gain; the trained classifier adds 10%. Most of the money is a policy change and an assignment problem — worth knowing before anyone funds the model. The capacity constraints’ dual values also say what one more unit of daily capacity at each site is worth, which turns the router into a where-to-invest list.

The network, capacities, recovery rates, costs and returns are synthetic, with ranges anchored to published industry figures. Every number is scored against each item’s true condition, which no model saw.

05

PolicyPulse — simulating customer backlash

500 simulated customers react to a return fee: casual shoppers, not bargain hunters, are the costly ones to lose.

Why

A spreadsheet says a $9.95 return fee saves millions. Then you announce it, people are angry online for a week, and some quietly never shop with you again. The spreadsheet knows what things cost, not how people react — and a survey doesn’t either, because backlash is decided by people talking to each other: one loud account posts, bargain hunters pile on, it snowballs.

How it works

  • Fees hurt in proportion. $9.95 stings on a $45 order and barely registers on a $165 one, and the pain levels off.
  • A crowd of 500 customers in five types — loyal, casual, bargain hunters, big followings, sustainability-minded — each reading a feed weighted toward loud accounts and like-minded people for 45 simulated days.
  • “Annoyed” and “leaving” are tracked separately, because plenty of people think a fee is unfair and keep buying.
  • Mood becomes money: fee savings against the value of customers lost, at every price point.
  • Two simulators kept honest against each other: a fast rule-based one (half a second a run) for exploring, and a slow one where every customer is an LLM writing real posts, to check the fast one.

What it found

Casual shoppers are worth more than bargain hunters, even though far fewer of them leave. Bargain hunters complain loudest and are the cheapest to lose. The two simulators rank which customer types are hit hardest similarly (rank correlation about 0.80) but disagree on exact numbers — which is expected, since the LLM version only sees what people chose to post.

The most useful bug: a diagnostic I wrote caught three different policies producing byte-identical results — the policy shock was being zeroed out. Fixing that, and speeding the fast simulator up 127× (70 s to 0.55 s per run), is what made the comparisons above mean anything.

“$12.95 return fee?? SERIOUSLY?? When my average order is $25–40, that’s 30–50% of what I spent!” — an LLM customer told only that it shops on a budget, arriving on its own at the same fee-versus-basket reasoning I’d hand-written into the fast model.

What America Is Buying — consumer data stories

Six stories from card, foot-traffic and receipt data — Walmart vs Target, Dunkin’ vs Starbucks, gyms, gas stations and more.

Why

Retail rivalries get explained with stereotypes — two Americas, two kinds of shopper. I wanted to check them against what people actually pay for and where they actually go.

How it works

One pipeline over licensed card-spending panels, foot-traffic data and convenience-store receipts (ConsumerEdge, SafeGraph, Advan, PDI via Dewey): source profiling, schema normalisation, resumable processing, 138 tests. Privacy rules are built in — aggregates only, minimum cohort sizes. Each story is written as a short article with every chain shown against the whole field.

What they found

  • Walmart vs Target — not different shoppers, different trips. 55% of Target’s card customers also bought at Walmart that month; a Walmart shopper spent $1,696 there in a year across 27 transactions, a Target shopper $573 across 11.
  • Home Depot vs Lowe’s — the national demographic gap mostly disappears inside the same county (2,361 counties, close to a coin toss). The business gap survives: 15% more visits per store in the same state.
  • Dunkin’ vs Starbucks — two coffee clocks. 31.8% of Dunkin’ visits fall between 5 and 10 a.m. (top 4% of 1,720 chains); Starbucks peaks at noon. Starbucks card reloads ($748M) now exceed direct café purchases ($682M), so card counts understate its visits.
  • Where America works out — February, not January, is the busiest gym month (about 9% above average). A Planet Fitness card customer spends about $21 a month, an Equinox customer $326.

Forecasting RDU flight delays the night before

Night-before delay forecasts rebuilt from 1.48M flights: 10.7% better Brier score than the best baseline, with leakage tests.

Why

Airline apps tell you about a delay a few hours before departure; at 11 PM the night before you know nothing. Is there real signal then, or do published studies claiming 85–90% accuracy quietly use information from the future?

How it works

A delay at Raleigh is often born somewhere else, so I rebuilt each aircraft’s day from 1.48 million national flights: which leg of the day your flight is, the slack between legs, where the plane spent last night, and weather forecasts for the airports on its route, frozen at 10:30 PM.

Most of the work was not cheating. My first model scored suspiciously well: it had learned that a blank tail number means a cancellation, but airlines blank it after they cancel. I removed every after-the-fact field, rebuilt the pipeline, and added automated leakage tests.

What it found

On a 2.5-year walk-forward backtest of 37,087 RDU flights, the full model improved delay-probability accuracy by 10.7% (Brier score) and minutes error by 14.7% (CRPS) over the best simple baseline, and its 80% ranges held about 80% of outcomes. A plane that arrived 30+ minutes late overnight about doubled morning delay risk.

Then I audited my own live site and found it wasn’t running the model the results described, and some missing outcomes were shown as “on time.” I fixed it. The public page now honestly runs the simple baseline every night and scores itself against real gate-out times; the full model goes live once its inputs work in real time.

12

WolfAttend

Two-factor class attendance (rotating code + campus WiFi) proposed to NC State IT — and the flaw their developer found.

Why

Attendance in a big lecture forces a choice between speed and honesty: calling names burns ten minutes, sign-in sheets get signed for friends, and a code on the projector gets texted to whoever’s still in bed. A professor and I kept coming back to it, so I designed an alternative and sent it to the university.

How it works

Two signals, because each alone is easy to beat. A rotating code proves you’re there now (beaten alone by a screenshot). The campus WiFi log — which access point your device is on, and which room that covers — proves you’re there physically (beaten alone by a VPN). Together they close both holes, with no new hardware or app. The output is only ever present or flagged for review; it never marks anyone absent automatically.

What happened

NC State’s IT leadership agreed it was a real problem, had a developer review it, and turned it down: the university is moving from Moodle to Canvas. The point that actually matters came from their developer — most students use cell data, not campus WiFi, so the presence signal often doesn’t exist. That breaks the design, and I haven’t found a fix.
