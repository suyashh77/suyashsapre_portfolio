Projects & Research

I'm fascinated by supply chains — less by the logistics than by the mathematics sitting underneath them. A truck leaving a dock half full, a warehouse built in the wrong place, a returned jacket crossing the country so somebody can look at it: every one of those is a decision made against a model, whether or not anyone wrote the model down. What interests me is writing it down — the formulation, the algorithm, and the point where the elegant version stops surviving contact with real operations.

Most of what follows is me building those models to find out where the difficulty actually lives.

R1

Google Maps Review Scraper — NC food access data

~84% collected

To be clear about credit: the food desert research is Teresa Gorton's and Dr. Rachunok's. I didn't do the research. I built the scraper and the data pipeline that made the collection possible.

Last semester Teresa Gorton and Dr. Rachunok were working on food desert research at NC State, and I got to help by building a Google Maps scraper. Most food desert research measures access in miles. They were looking at it through Penchansky & Thomas's five dimensions of access — availability, accessibility, accommodation, affordability, acceptability — measured through what people actually write in Google Maps reviews. That approach had been demonstrated for Raleigh; the job was to scale it to the whole state.

We ended up covering nearly 5,000 grocery stores across almost 600 NC cities, pulling in over 1.2 million reviews along the way.

How it works

Two stages. First, resolve a universe of stores: 451,296 Overture Maps POIs down to North Carolina, enriched to Google place_ids, fuzzy-matched and manually reviewed, then filtered from 424 raw Google categories down to the 13 that are actually grocery. Second, scrape reviews for the survivors — headless Playwright on Linux VMs, running continuously, restarting itself on crash and draining a batch until it's done. 

Overture Maps US POIs                     451,296
 └─ NC filter                             16,680
    └─ GMaps enrichment (place_id)        7,858 matched + 6,607 unmatched
       └─ Outscraper second pass          21,840 results
          └─ Fuzzy match + manual review  4,905 matched / 1,605 rejected
             └─ Category filter           424 GMaps categories → 13 in, 411 out
                └─ Closed-store + place_id dedup
                   └─ Review scraping     1,201,237 reviews

Every dropped store is kept with a machine-readable reason in a 7,497-row exclusion audit, and every review carries a provenance tag for the batch it came from. A filtered-out store is a research finding, not a deleted row — without that trail nobody downstream can tell whether a coverage gap is a real food desert or a scraper failure.

What actually broke

The scraping was the easy part. What took months was everything that broke.

  • Lost an 8-hour run to a Wi-Fi blip — moved everything to SQLite, so it now resumes mid-store instead of restarting, right down to which review sort order it was part-way through.
  • Silently scraped the wrong Dollar General once — built a geo-matcher to check the store name against haversine distance from the expected coordinates before trusting anything. Not a strict distance reject, though: Overture coordinates are routinely 1–2 km off Google's even for the correct store, and a tight threshold wrongly killed ~25 valid stores.
  • Burned Google accounts treating CAPTCHAs and sign-in walls as the same problem — one blocks your IP, the other blocks your account. A sign-in wall means ban that account and rotate; a CAPTCHA means write a global pause flag and sleep 30–60 minutes.
  • Trusted an in-memory counter for reviews_scraped and got 2,263 phantom reviews. The count is now re-read from disk after every flush, so progress can never drift from what was actually persisted.
  • Assumed a scroll stall meant the end of the list. It doesn't — Google stops yielding long before you've seen everything, so the scraper re-sorts newest → oldest → relevant, because different sort orders surface different subsets of the same reviews.

What I'd tell myself at the start

  • Resumability is the whole game. An 8-hour run that loses everything is worth zero. State belongs in SQLite, not memory.
  • Clean abstractions can be the wrong abstractions. The first version was a tidy config-driven package; one selector change on Google's side meant edits across three files. It's in Archive/ along with three other architectures that didn't survive production.
  • Parallelism wasn't the bottleneck. I fixed SQLite contention with WAL and immediate-mode store claiming, then found Google's rate limits were the real ceiling all along.
  • Shipping it to other people is a design spec. A Tkinter GUI build let non-technical classmates run batches on their own laptops — it contributed 19,992 reviews and 25 batch DBs, and every complaint they filed (leaked tabs, one bad store halting the batch, no checkpointing) became a requirement for the next version.

Output and honesty

4,889 stores · 1,201,237 reviews · 578 NC cities · 13 categories. It's about 84% done, and every gap is documented in the repo's quality report rather than buried — the text corpus is really ~608K reviews because 49.4% of Google reviews are rating-only; 157 stores were over-scraped by the wrong-location bug before the geo guardrail existed; Costco is missing entirely. The path from "usable" to "authoritative" is itemised and effort-estimated in the future-work doc.

Thank you to Dr. Ben Rachunok and everyone in the lab — the weekly discussions taught me as much as building this did.

R2

GeoMatch

Shipped · v1.0

The wrong-Dollar-General problem, generalised into a library. The same physical place looks different in every data source: "Food Lion #1542, 2410 Wade Ave Ste 100", "Food Lion, Wade Avenue, Raleigh" and "FOOD LION 2410 WADE AVE RALEIGH NC 27607" are one store, three records, zero exact matches. GeoMatch links them in one function call — offline, no API keys, nothing leaves your machine.

The hard part

Being explainable and un-opinionated at the same time. Every match returns a calibrated probability and a plain-English reason — "accepted by rule: house number + street + ZIP/city all match". And link never auto-filters: you get every candidate pair with its full feature row, veto flag, and parsed components for both sides, and you pick the threshold. A matcher that silently drops pairs is unusable for research, because the drops are exactly what you need to audit.

  • Seven layers: normalise → parse → hard rules → similarity features → scoring → calibration → explanation.
  • A token state machine pulls house number, street, suffix, unit, city, state, ZIP and POI name using a pure-Python USPS Pub 28 expander — no libpostal, so it installs anywhere.
  • Non-negotiable identifiers veto or accept before any scoring: a ZIP / house-number / state mismatch rejects outright; a matching identifier triple accepts outright.
  • Optional ONNX transformer features (~15MB, no PyTorch) when names need semantic comparison.
  • Also exposes dedupe and standardize, plus a pandas .geo accessor.

The end-to-end idea

Several of these aren't separate projects so much as separate layers of one system. Real supply chains decide on wildly different clocks — where to put a building (months to years), which truck to load tonight (days), what route it drives (hours), what comes back (continuously) — and each layer's decision quietly constrains the next one down. Most tools solve exactly one layer and assume the others away. I want to build the version that doesn't. 

Strategic     Where do facilities go?         →  03  facility location
Tactical      What goes on which truck?      →  04  consolidation
Operational   What route does it drive?      →      routing / TSP & VRP
Reverse       Where does it go coming back?  →  01  return routing

The interesting part isn't the layers, it's the seams

  • A facility-location model that ignores consolidation will site warehouses that are expensive to actually serve, because it prices lanes as if every shipment were a full truck.
  • Load building that ignores the route it feeds will build trucks that are cube-efficient and route-hostile.
  • And forward network design that ignores returns undersizes exactly the nodes the return router later discovers are the binding constraint — the capacity duals in project 01 are already a signal pointing back up at project 03.

So the plan is to build the missing middle, then connect the layers and measure the thing nobody reports: how much value is lost at the handoffs when each layer is optimised alone. That number is the whole argument for end-to-end.

01

Return Routing Optimisation

Results complete Write-up in progress

I returned a North Face jacket. It was unworn — wrong size, sent back within a few days — and it still got shipped across the country to a facility so somebody could open the box and decide it was resellable. Everything needed to make that call was knowable before the box moved. That's the whole project: decide where a return goes before it ships, not after.

Most retailers route every return to a central warehouse, inspect it there, and only then decide what to do with it. Deciding after the item has already travelled wastes the shipping and lets value decay; the industry loses an estimated 10–20% of a product's value this way. This system decides the destination at the moment the return label is generated, using the expected recovery value of each possible route across a capacitated network.

How it works

  • L1 — predicts a probability distribution over the item's condition from label-time features only.
  • L2 — prices every route in expected dollars: EV(j) = Σₖ P(k) × [R(j,k) − T(j) − P(j,k)]
  • L3 — an integer program assigns the whole day's returns under every facility's capacity limit.

Results

Policy Net recovery / return Cost / return A-items at full price
P0 — centralised (strawman) $39.46 $15.07 0%
PNEAR — nearest DC (baseline) $40.46 $13.63 0%
P1 — EV routing, mail only $41.57 $11.70 0%
P2 — EV routing + free in-store $45.66 $9.70 29.4%
Oracle — perfect condition info $46.75 $8.10 30.7%

+$5.20 recovered per return over the credible baseline — about $520,000/year at 100,000 returns. Paired over the same returns: 95% bootstrap CI [+$4.92, +$5.48], t = 36.3, Cohen's d = 0.57, with 61.9% of returns routed differently. Using label-time features alone, it captures 83% of the value a perfect-information router would achieve.

The part worth volunteering before you're asked

Most of that is not the machine learning. The Layer-1 quality sweep doubles as a decomposition: at skill = 0 the classifier is switched off entirely and every return gets the population base rate, so whatever survives is earned by the channel change and the optimiser alone. Free in-store returns plus capacity-aware routing account for +$4.68 — 90% of the uplift. The trained condition classifier accounts for +$0.51 — 10%.

Most of the money is a policy change and an assignment problem. Leading with the classifier because it is the most technically interesting part would be the easiest and least honest thing to do with these results. It also sharpens the roadmap: of the value attributable to condition information at all, the model captures 34% — the rest needs better data, not a better model, which is the case for capturing customer photos at return initiation (~$98k/year at the same volume).

The second output is a capacity plan. The dual on each capacity constraint says what one more unit of daily throughput at that node is worth, which turns a router into a where-to-invest recommendation.

The North Face anecdote is motivation only — it contributed no data. The network, capacities, recovery rates, costs, and the returns themselves are synthetic; parameter ranges are anchored to published industry figures, and no value is claimed to be measured from any real company. Every figure above is scored against the item's true condition, which no model ever saw.

02

Supply Chain Risk Intelligence

Rebuilding next

Converts real-time news into structured risk indices and alerts so supply chain leaders can anticipate disruptions instead of reacting to them. Multi-source ingestion via NewsAPI, NLP + GPT risk scoring, corridor and port indices, executive heatmaps, and export to CSV / PDF / Power BI.

Why it's getting rebuilt

The scoring layer is an LLM asked to rate a headline, which is not a risk model — no calibration, no base rate, no way for it to be wrong in a measurable direction. The version that exists is a convincing demo of the wrong thing.

The rebuild replaces it with event extraction against a defined taxonomy plus an explicit exposure map, so an index number traces back to a named supply node rather than to a vibe — and so the whole thing can be scored against disruptions that actually happened. This is the next one I'm working on.

03

Facility Location — Greedy-Add Solver

Shipped

Where should the warehouses go, and how much does it cost you to get that wrong? An interactive solver for the Uncapacitated Fixed-Charge Location Problem using the Greedy-Add construction heuristic — 100 randomly generated US customers with demand, and 1–10 candidate facilities you place by street address.

I worked on this class of problem during my co-op rotations at UPL, on real warehouse network questions. This is not that work — it's the same problem rebuilt from scratch on synthetic data, so there's something I can actually show.

The hard part

Making the heuristic legible. It renders every iteration as a table — which facility was evaluated, what it saved, why it won — next to a map of the resulting assignment arcs. The point isn't the optimum; it's watching a greedy method commit to a facility early and seeing exactly where that costs you.

Consolidation Centres & Load Building

Planned

I've always wondered how Lowe's and companies at that scale actually run consolidation centres and cross-dock terminals — how freight from hundreds of suppliers gets pooled, and how anyone decides what goes on which truck. It looks like a scheduling problem and a packing problem wearing the same coat, and I want to build it to find out where the real difficulty lives.

The questions I want to answer

  • When is it worth holding freight one more day to build a fuller truck, and what does that deferral cost downstream in service?
  • How do you assign inbound loads to outbound doors and departure windows when both dock capacity and trailer cube are binding?
  • Where does a consolidation centre stop paying for itself — at what volume, distance, and supplier fragmentation does direct-ship win?

Nothing built yet. This entry is here because it's the next thing I want to work on, and because it's the missing middle of the end-to-end system below.

05

PolicyPulse

Finishing now

A multi-agent consumer simulation for pre-launch retail policy validation, built as the companion to the return routing system above. The optimiser says what to do economically; PolicyPulse asks the question an EV calculator structurally cannot — will consumers actually accept it? A policy that is financially optimal but behaviourally unacceptable drives churn and backlash that never shows up in the objective function.

The hard part

The claim being tested is that emergent group dynamics — the pile-on, the counter-narrative, the influencer amplification — are fundamentally different from the sum of individual stated preferences. Focus groups and surveys capture the latter. So the simulation has to be capable of producing cascades rather than averages, which means agents need persistent memory and a feed algorithm, not just a good prompt.

  • Takes a proposed policy change, generates 500 agents across five retail personas, and simulates 45 days of reaction.
  • Outputs a sentiment trajectory, churn probability by segment, cascade detection, fee-threshold comparison across runs, and an agent interview explaining individual decisions.
  • Built on MiroFish / OASIS (CAMEL-AI), with a Neo4j knowledge graph for brand ↔ policy ↔ segment ↔ competitor relationships and Zep Cloud for per-agent persistent memory.
StackClaude Sonnet · OASIS / MiroFish · Neo4j · Zep Cloud · FastAPI · Streamlit · PRAW · Ollama (Qwen2.5-32B) fallback
StatusSpec and simulation core are written; the report layer and validation runs are what's left. No results yet, and none claimed.

Why it's being reworked

07

Garmin Wrapped

Shipped

Turning roughly 500,000 data points from a Garmin watch into a personalised annual health story. Every year friends post their Spotify Wrapped; Garmin shows me records and a step-count bar chart — no story, no curation. So I built the narrative layer.

The hard part

Not computing insights — rejecting them. Rather than showing everything (a dashboard), it answers one question: what are the most interesting things your data revealed this year? Every candidate insight passes two gates before it reaches you — is there enough data to make this claim reliably, and is this fact actually worth one of your ten slides? The survivors are then ordered into a Freytag dramatic arc rather than by score.

  • Prophet-based decomposition for genuine anomaly detection, not "low step day".
  • Granger-gated cross-metric insights — sleep vs. next-day activity, tested before it's claimed.
  • Population comparisons anchored in peer-reviewed epidemiological studies; personality archetypes like Weekend Warrior, Steady Pacer, Cool Engine.
  • gpt-4o for climax slides, gpt-4o-mini for context slides, template fallback when there's no API key.

08

WolfAttend

Proposal submitted — OIT & DELTA

A frictionless, verified attendance system for NC State. Every existing option makes you choose between friction (roll call, QR queues) and fraud (a code your friend texts you from home). WolfAttend removes the choice by binding the code to the network the student is actually standing on.

The hard part

This one is a threat model, not a feature list. A rotating TOTP session code alone is defeated by screenshot sharing; WiFi presence alone is defeated by VPN spoofing. Validating the TOTP against a Cisco ISE pxGrid AP-to-room map closes both at once, and replay dies with the time window. The constraints mattered more than the mechanism: zero new hardware, zero student apps, never auto-mark anyone absent, FERPA-compliant by construction. 

Moodle layer          →  session start, TOTP issue, roster write-back
Validation layer      →  TOTP check × live client-to-AP association
Cisco ISE (pxGrid)    →  AP-to-room map, device presence
