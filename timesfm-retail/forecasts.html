Can an already trained AI model help a grocery store forecast sales?

This study compares Google's TimesFM 3.0 on four product groups at one grocery store with repeating last week's sales and with a machine-learning model fitted to earlier sales.

The result: With sales and calendar inputs, TimesFM scored 9.68% WAPE and gradient boosting scored 12.30%. Calendar information helped TimesFM. Adding promotions and past transactions did not improve that version in this test.

SITUATION AND TASK

A store has to order before it knows what will sell.

Too little stock can leave shelves empty. Too much stock can increase holding costs and waste. A useful forecast helps decide what to order.

TimesFM has already learned patterns from other time-series data. The question was whether it could provide useful sales forecasts here without training a new forecasting model on this dataset.

The data comes from Favorita store 1 in Quito, Ecuador. The targets are beverages, bread/bakery, dairy, and grocery I.

These are product groups, rather than individual products. Recorded sales measure quantity, sometimes including fractions. They do not tell us everything customers would have bought if the store had always had stock.

ANALYSIS

Give each method the past, then check its next two weeks.

Both workflows had access to 256 days of local sales history and predicted the next 14 days. The test covers twelve weekly starting dates in 2017. Later sales and transaction counts were hidden until they became part of the past.

The three pairs use sales only, sales plus calendar, or sales plus calendar, promotions and past transactions. All paired workflows can use all four product groups. Gradient boosting is fitted within the local window; TimesFM uses unchanged pretrained weights.

What each method received

The weekly rule repeats the previous week. Both paired sales-only models receive four target histories. Both calendar models also receive weekday, annual position, holiday and payday signals. Both full-input models additionally receive the four promotion series and historical store transaction counts. Boosting summarizes the histories with 28-day features and uses earlier in-window labels. TimesFM processes the histories directly.

One assumption matters: future promotions were treated as already planned. The source does not preserve dated plans, so that availability cannot be verified. Future transactions were never used. Prices are absent from this dataset.

RESULT: FORECASTS

Compare the three pairs given the same local inputs.

The score adds up the size of all forecast mistakes and divides by total recorded sales. TimesFM with calendar information made 22.9% less total error than the weekly rule in this test.

Where it helped, and where it struggled.

Results varied by product group.

With sales and calendar inputs, TimesFM did better on beverages, bread/bakery and grocery I. Gradient boosting did better on dairy. With all inputs, boosting did better on three groups, while TimesFM did better on grocery I, which represents about 45% of the scored quantity.

Considering the groups together helped only a little. Adding promotions and past transactions made the calendar version's total error worse. This test changes those two inputs together, so it cannot explain which one mattered.

Some individual days were far off.

For August 11, a forecast made on August 2 predicted about 2,120 beverage units. The recorded quantity was 1,006. That observation also fell below the estimated middle-80% range.

The range is an estimate for each day. Real sales can fall outside it. This project documents the miss but does not establish what caused it.

How useful were the uncertainty ranges?

Calendar-aware TimesFM's estimated middle-80% range covered 87.8% of observations. Calendar-aware gradient boosting's covered 83.3%. A wider range can cover more outcomes, so coverage alone cannot identify the better uncertainty estimate.

TimesFM's average range was slightly wider: 574 versus 562 sales quantity units. Its pinball loss, which scores all nine predicted quantiles, was lower: 56.78 versus 72.82. These observations do not establish reliable calibration on new data. These pooled measures also reflect differences in product-group size.

RESULT: A SIMPLE INVENTORY SIMULATION

Would the forecasts lead to useful stocking decisions?

The inventory replay simulates weekly orders with a seven-day delivery time. Each policy orders enough for its next fourteen-day sales forecast, plus extra stock estimated from earlier mistakes. Every method starts with the same stock.

TimesFM with the groups together had the lowest cost under the default assumptions: 20.4% below the weekly rule. It fulfilled 99.73% of recorded sales quantity, compared with 98.61% for that rule. This is a simulation using recorded sales as a substitute for demand.

The lowest forecast error and the lowest simulated inventory cost came from different TimesFM versions. The inventory policy uses errors in earlier fourteen-day totals to estimate extra stock. It does not use TimesFM's daily uncertainty bands directly.

What this experiment supports.

TimesFM was a useful forecasting candidate on this store and period, without task-specific training. Its pooled error was lower in the three matched-local-data pairs. Giving the corrected calendar boosting workflow 365 days of history improved its WAPE to 10.82%, showing why history budgets matter. The extra inputs did not all help.

This covers one store and four product groups. The comparison does not establish which model works best across retail. ARIMA and exponential smoothing were not tested. The public data could overlap Google's earlier training data.

Inventory costs are simulated. The replay leaves out expiry, capacity, buying costs, and the value of remaining stock at the end. Different costs can change which policy looks best. The downloadable TimesFM 3.0 weights also carry a non-commercial, non-production license.
